For injured patients

If you think something is broken, here is what happens next.

Most fractures are not emergencies measured in minutes — but a few are. The steps on the right are the ones I'd want my own family to follow.

Call 911 now if
The limb is cold, pale or numb below the injury · bone is visible through the skin · the injury followed a fall from height, a car or motorcycle crash, or a pedestrian strike · there is a head injury, chest pain or trouble breathing alongside the fracture.
  1. 01

    Don't test it. Splint it and stop moving.

    If a limb is deformed, won't bear weight, or a joint won't move, assume it's fractured until an X-ray says otherwise. Keep it still with whatever is at hand — a rolled magazine, a pillow, a folded towel — and elevate it above the heart if you can.

  2. 02

    Get to an emergency department, not an urgent care.

    Hip, pelvis, femur and open fractures need a hospital with orthopaedic surgery on call. Urgent care can X-ray a wrist; it cannot admit you or take you to the operating room. If you cannot put weight through the leg, use an ambulance.

  3. 03

    Bring three things: your medication list, your last meal time, and your questions.

    Blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, clopidogrel) change the timing of surgery. Anaesthesia needs to know when you last ate or drank — for most fractures needing an operation, that clock starts now, so stop eating once you suspect a break.

  4. 04

    Expect imaging, a splint or traction, and a plan within the first day.

    X-rays first, a CT scan if the fracture involves a joint or the pelvis. Most hip fractures are operated on within 24 to 48 hours of admission because earlier surgery means earlier standing and fewer complications. The surgeon should be able to tell you what broke, how it will be fixed, and when you'll be allowed to bear weight.

  5. 05

    Recovery is measured in weeks, and you are the most important part of it.

    Bone heals on a schedule — roughly 6 weeks for the first solid bridge, 3 to 6 months to full strength for a femur. Physical therapy, nutrition (protein and vitamin D), not smoking, and showing up to every follow-up X-ray move that timeline more than anything I do in the operating room.

Understand your injury · Hip and femur

The fractures I treat most, in plain language.

The femur is the longest bone in the body and it breaks in four characteristic places. Pick the one on your X-ray report. Each guide describes what usually happens — your surgeon's plan for you is the one that counts.

Femoral neck fracture

A break in the narrow bridge of bone just below the ball of the hip joint, inside the joint capsule.

AO/OTA 31-BGarden I–IVPauwels I–III

What broke

The femoral head (the ball) sits on a short neck of bone. The blood supply to the head runs up along that neck, so when the neck breaks and shifts, the head can lose its blood supply. That single fact drives almost every decision about this fracture. In older adults it usually follows a fall from standing height; in younger adults it takes a much higher-energy injury.

How it is usually fixed

If the fracture has not moved (Garden I or II), the neck can often be held with screws or a small sliding plate while it heals in place. If it has displaced (Garden III or IV) in an older patient, replacing the ball — a hemiarthroplasty — or the whole joint — a total hip arthroplasty — is usually more reliable than trying to make the bone heal, because it lets you stand on the leg the next day and removes the risk of the head dying later. In a younger patient the priority flips: an urgent, anatomic reduction and fixation to try to save the native hip.

Recovery

After a replacement you will typically walk with full weight-bearing on day one and be home or in rehab within 2 to 4 days. After screw fixation, weight-bearing is often restricted for 6 to 12 weeks while the neck heals, with X-rays at 2, 6 and 12 weeks and again at a year to check the head is alive. The most important thing you can do in the first month is get up and move — pneumonia, blood clots and pressure sores are the enemies of a hip fracture, not the incision.

Note
Whether a displaced femoral neck fracture does better with fixation or a replacement is the subject of my own research. The honest answer is that it depends on your age, your activity level, your bone quality and how far the fracture has moved — bring those four things up with your surgeon.

Intertrochanteric hip fracture

A break through the broad part of the upper femur, between the two bony prominences below the neck, outside the joint capsule.

AO/OTA 31-AStable vs. unstableReverse obliquity

What broke

This is the most common hip fracture in older adults. Because it sits outside the joint capsule the blood supply to the femoral head is preserved, so the bone heals well — the challenge is holding it in position while it does. The fracture is called "stable" when the inner cortex of bone still lines up and "unstable" when the back-inner wall is broken into pieces or the fracture line runs the wrong way (reverse obliquity).

How it is usually fixed

Almost all of these are fixed, not replaced. A stable pattern can be held with a sliding hip screw and short plate. An unstable pattern is usually held with a cephalomedullary nail — a rod placed down the inside of the femur with a large screw passing up into the femoral head. The nail lets you put full weight through the leg immediately, which for an older patient is the whole point.

Recovery

Standing with the physical therapist on day one, walking with a walker within the first few days, and 6 to 12 weeks until the fracture is solid on X-ray. Most people recover most of their walking ability, but roughly a third need a walking aid they didn't need before. Bone-strengthening medication after a hip fracture roughly halves the chance of a second one — ask about it before you leave the hospital.

Femoral shaft fracture

A break through the long, tubular middle of the thigh bone.

AO/OTA 32Winquist 0–IVOpen · Gustilo I–III

What broke

The shaft of the femur is the strongest bone in the body, so breaking it takes real force — a car or motorcycle crash, a fall from height, a sporting collision — and it is often not the only injury. The thigh can hold more than a litre of blood, and a shaft fracture is treated as a whole-body injury first: airway, breathing, circulation, then the femur.

How it is usually fixed

The standard operation is an intramedullary nail: a titanium rod passed down the hollow centre of the bone, locked with screws at each end, through incisions a few centimetres long. It is usually done within 24 hours once you are stable. If the skin is broken (an open fracture), you'll get antibiotics within the first hour and a washout in the operating room before or during the nailing.

Recovery

A nailed femur is strong enough to bear weight early — often immediately, sometimes with a walker for the first weeks. Expect 3 to 4 months to a healed X-ray and 6 to 12 months to full strength and sport. A femur is a big bone to heal; protein, not smoking, and your physical therapy schedule are the levers you control.

Pitfall
Roughly 1 in 20 femoral shaft fractures has a second, hidden fracture in the femoral neck of the same leg. It is easy to miss on the first X-ray and much harder to treat once it has displaced. Every shaft fracture I treat gets dedicated hip imaging before, during and after the nail — ask your team to confirm the neck has been checked.

Distal femur fracture

A break through the flared lower end of the femur, just above — and sometimes into — the knee joint.

AO/OTA 33Extra- vs. intra-articular

What broke

This fracture has two very different faces. In older adults with thinner bone it follows a simple fall and often occurs above a knee replacement. In younger adults it is a high-energy injury, and the fracture frequently splits the joint surface into pieces (intra-articular). Where the fracture runs relative to the joint surface decides how it is treated and how the knee moves afterward.

How it is usually fixed

The two main tools are a long locking plate along the outer side of the femur and a retrograde nail passed up from the knee. When the joint surface is broken, the pieces are rebuilt and held with screws first, then the whole block is attached back to the shaft. This is a fracture where surgeons argue about the right implant — and where getting the length, rotation and alignment right matters more than which metal is chosen.

Recovery

Knee motion starts within days, because a stiff knee is the most common lasting problem. Weight-bearing is usually limited for 6 to 12 weeks, with X-rays at 2, 6 and 12 weeks. Distal femur fractures are slower to heal than shaft fractures — 4 to 6 months is typical — and some need a second procedure to encourage healing.

Fractures around a hip or knee replacement

A break in the femur next to — or between — the metal of an existing joint replacement.

PeriprostheticVancouver A–CInterprosthetic

What broke

Bone next to an implant carries stress differently, and after a replacement the femur can fracture around the stem after a fall that would otherwise cause a bruise. When someone has both a hip and a knee replacement in the same leg, the bone between them can break — an interprosthetic fracture — which is one of the harder problems in fracture surgery and the subject of a multi-centre study I'm part of.

How it is usually fixed

The first question is whether the implant is still firmly attached to the bone. If it is, the fracture is fixed around it with a long plate, cables or a combination. If the stem has loosened, the replacement itself has to be revised to a longer stem that reaches past the fracture. These are longer operations with more blood loss, done by surgeons who do both fracture and revision joint surgery.

Recovery

Expect a slower course than a straightforward hip fracture: a longer hospital stay, often a period of protected weight-bearing, and 4 to 6 months to a healed X-ray. Because these patients are usually older and have had previous surgery, the medical side of recovery — heart, lungs, kidneys, nutrition — deserves as much attention as the bone.

For residents

Read the mechanism before you read the X-ray.

Most fracture questions — on the board exam and at 2 a.m. — are answered by asking how the energy got into the bone and where it had to go. Below is the framework I teach on the trauma service, and the two mistakes I see most.

Five questions for every fracture

Consult to plan
  1. Mechanism and energy. Fall from standing or motorcycle at 60 mph? Energy predicts soft-tissue injury, associated fractures and how much the bone will tolerate.
  2. Host. Age, bone quality, diabetes, smoking, anticoagulation, baseline function. The same X-ray gets a different operation in a 34-year-old and an 84-year-old.
  3. Soft-tissue envelope. Open or closed, skin at risk, compartment pressures, pulses. The skin decides the timing; the bone decides the implant.
  4. Personality of the fracture. Articular or extra-articular, comminution, the key fragment that must be reduced first, the deforming forces acting on it.
  5. Goal and the implant that serves it. Absolute stability for the joint surface, relative stability for the shaft, immediate weight-bearing for the elderly hip. Choose the goal, then the metal.
Pitfall
The ipsilateral femoral neck fracture behind a femoral shaft fracture. Reported in roughly 2–9% of shaft fractures; missed on the initial trauma series in a large share of those. Dedicated AP internal-rotation hip radiograph, fine-cut CT of the neck, and a fluoroscopic look at the neck before and after the nail. Assume it is there until you have proven it is not.
Pearl
In a displaced femoral neck fracture in a physiologically young patient, the clock is the reduction, not the incision. An anatomic reduction at 18 hours beats a poor one at 6. Get the traction table, get the C-arm in both planes, and don't accept varus.
Evidence
For displaced femoral neck fractures in patients over 50, the HEALTH trial (Bhandari et al., NEJM, 2019) found no difference in secondary procedures at 24 months between total hip and hemiarthroplasty — a reminder that the fixation-versus-replacement decision matters more than the choice of replacement.

The follow-up film at six weeks is the most honest teacher in orthopaedics. Learn to want it.
